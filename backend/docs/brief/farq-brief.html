FARQ

A WhatsApp service that checks a suspicious financial video against its sources before you act on it, and tells you in plain words what it found.

Amazon AI Cyber Security Hackathon finale Status as of 24 Oct 2026
The problem

Investment scams that borrow real faces and real voices

Scammers in India circulate videos of public figures, such as the Finance Minister or the RBI Governor, appearing to recommend an investment scheme. PIB Fact Check has reported that recent fakes are collages: the official's real face, a voice taken from other real videos, and AI used only to re-sync the lips (team to attach the PIB source).

Because most of the pixels and most of the voice are genuine, a detector that looks for synthetic pixels has little to find. Our working hypothesis, which the demo and our test set will check, is that generic detectors often pass these videos.

The idea

Verify against sources, not only pixels

Instead of asking only "does this look fake?", FARQ asks four questions and shows the evidence for each:

Q1 · Source map

Where did every second come from?

Match video segments against official footage, and spoken phrases against the person's real speeches, with timestamps.

Q2 · Original + diff

What changed from the original?

Align the found original, compare the mouth with the rest of the face, and diff the transcripts.

Q3 · Binding

Do this voice and this body belong together?

Check whether vocal emphasis lines up with head and eyebrow movement, ignoring the mouth.

Q4 · Supporting signals

What else points to a scam?

Known-fake matches, scam-script matches, official warning lists, and phone numbers, UPI IDs and links shown or spoken.

An investigator agent (Claude on Amazon Bedrock) then writes a short report from these findings. Every sentence cites an evidence ID. The agent never sets the verdict.

How a check works

From a forwarded video to a verdict

  1. The user forwards the video, voice note or screenshot to the FARQ bot on WhatsApp (Telegram as a second channel).
  2. Three fixed buttons ask who it claims to be, what it asks for, and whether money was already sent. If money was sent, the reply leads with call 1930 (National Cyber Crime Helpline) and bank steps.
  3. Fast lookup: if a reviewer has already confirmed this exact video, the answer comes back immediately.
  4. Extract: on-screen text first (OCR), then speech-to-text with word timings.
  5. Verify: source map, original and diff, official lists.
  6. Analyse: audio-visual binding and an audio deepfake score, each turned into a level (low, medium or high).
  7. Decide: a rules file sets the verdict from the evidence. Weak or conflicting evidence gives NOT VERIFIED.
  8. Reply: the verdict, two to four plain reasons, next steps, and a timeline image. On request, a pre-filled complaint and an evidence PDF for the user to file on cybercrime.gov.in.
CONFIRMED SCAM · only after human review LIKELY SCAM · goes to a reviewer NOT VERIFIED · never "genuine"

The demo uses a consenting team member as the protected person. Test fakes are made on-site from team clips only.

Novelty

What is new, and what it builds on

1. Source attribution instead of a single fake score

FARQ explains where each second of video and each spoken phrase came from. For collage fakes, the strongest evidence is a real source used out of context, and that evidence can be checked by anyone.

Builds on: the manual reverse-search work fact-checkers already do. New for us: doing it automatically, per second, for both picture and speech, in Indian languages.

2. Locating the lip-sync edit

Once the original is found and aligned, compression changes the whole face about equally, while lip-sync concentrates change in the mouth. Comparing the two gives "lips altered 0:04–0:39", plus a word-level diff of what was said.

3. Mouth-blind audio-visual binding

The attacker optimises the mouth to match the new audio. We deliberately ignore the mouth and test what they don't optimise: whether vocal emphasis (pitch and loudness) moves with head nods and eyebrow raises, calibrated against the person's own real clips.

Builds on: Agarwal and Farid, "Protecting World Leaders Against Deep Fakes" (CVPR Workshops 2019), which modelled a person's facial and head mannerisms; and Chugh et al., "Not made for each other" (ACM Multimedia 2020), on audio-visual mismatch. Difference: we exclude the forged region and target the coupling between voice and upper-face movement.

4. The knockout test

When the original is found, we pair the fake's body movement with the original audio. If the coupling jumps, the body belongs to other words. It is a cause-and-effect check that a non-expert audience can watch happen.

5. Evidence decides; the AI only explains

A rules file sets the verdict; the language model only narrates, and must cite evidence IDs. FARQ abstains when evidence is weak, never calls a video genuine, and only says CONFIRMED after a person reviews it.

6. Built around the victim's next step

It works inside WhatsApp, where these videos spread. If money was sent, 1930 comes first. It produces a police-ready evidence pack that the user files themselves, and it groups repeat forwards of the same fake into campaigns.

Novelty claims 1, 2 and 4 are our assessment; a literature check before the final slides is on our to-do list.

Safety and honesty rules

How FARQ avoids doing harm

  • Never decides on a single weak signal; strong signals must come from two different kinds of source.
  • Never auto-blocks or auto-reports; the user stays in control.
  • Shows reasons and a level (low, medium, high), never raw scores. Levels are percentiles against the person's own genuine clips, worded honestly for the number of clips we have.
  • Every verdict and reviewer action is logged: who, when, which evidence, which model versions.
  • Data: public, synthetic or consented team data only. Phone numbers are stored only as keyed hashes.

Limits

What FARQ can't do yet

  • The source map only finds sources that are in our library. A fake built from footage we don't have falls back to binding and the supporting signals.
  • Per-person calibration needs genuine clips of that person. For people outside the library, levels are less certain.
  • A fully synthetic video, with no reused footage, removes the source evidence; binding and the audio head still apply.
  • An attacker could learn to fake head and eyebrow movement that matches the voice. Binding raises the cost; it does not end the arms race.
  • Transcription quality on mixed Hindi and English is unmeasured until M2 testing.
For study

Questions a judge may ask

Why not just use a deepfake classifier?

Collage fakes are mostly real pixels and real voice, so a classifier has little synthetic signal to find. We test the relationships between the parts instead: their sources, whether they were recorded together, and what changed. We'll show a generic detector on the same fake in the demo, using a real run.

What if the original isn't in your library?

Then binding, the audio score, scam-script matching and the warning lists still run. If the evidence stays weak, FARQ says NOT VERIFIED, lists any warning signs, and gives the helpline. It never guesses and never says "genuine".

Couldn't the AI make a wrong accusation?

The language model can't set the verdict. A rules file does, from logged evidence, and CONFIRMED needs a human reviewer. The model only writes the explanation and must cite evidence IDs.

How accurate is it?

We don't know yet, and we won't claim a number before we measure one. Results will come from the M6 test set and will be reported with its size.

What about privacy?

Phone numbers are stored as keyed hashes, never in raw form. We use only public, synthetic or consented team data. Media and models stay on our machine; the agent works from structured findings.

Why does it ignore the mouth?

The mouth is the part the attacker forged to match the new audio, so it will match. Head nods and eyebrow raises are left over from the original recording and follow the original words, not the new ones.

Glossary

Deepfake
Video or audio altered or generated with AI to show someone saying something they didn't.
Lip-sync
Re-animating only the mouth so it matches different audio.
Perceptual hash
A fingerprint of an image or video that stays similar when the file is re-compressed or resized (vpdq, pdqhash).
SHA-256
An exact fingerprint of a file; any change gives a different value. Used for exact matches and for the evidence PDF.
Embedding
A list of numbers that represents meaning, so similar phrases end up close together, even across languages (BGE-M3).
OCR
Optical character recognition: reading text from an image.
VAD
Voice activity detection: finding where someone is speaking.
Cross-correlation
Measuring how well two signals move together, including when one is slightly delayed (up to ±300 ms here).
Percentile
Where a value sits among the person's real clips, for example "less in sync than 97% of them".
Abstain
Declining to give a verdict when the evidence is weak; shown as NOT VERIFIED.
Frozen backbone
A pre-trained model used as-is to produce features, with only a small part trained on top.
